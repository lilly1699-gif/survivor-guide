After Childhood Cancer: A Guide for Young Adult Survivors in Massachusetts

If you had cancer as a child and are now a young adult in Massachusetts, this free guide can help you understand why follow-up care matters, find a survivorship clinic, get your treatment records, find money for school and living costs, and connect with other survivors. As a survivor myself, I always struggled to find information specific to young adults who survived childhood cancer, which is why I created this guide to hopefully help others.

Why follow-up care matters

Cancer treatment you had as a child can cause health problems years or even decades later. Doctors call these late effects. Blood Cancer United reports that 80% of childhood cancer survivors have chronic health issues from treatment (Blood Cancer United (opens in a new tab)).

The good news: many late effects can be caught early with the right screenings. Which screenings you need depends on the exact treatment you had, which is why your treatment summary matters so much. You might not remember your treatment if you were young, but survivorship care is meant to be lifelong (M Health Fairview (opens in a new tab)).

Getting your treatment records

The goal is a treatment summary: one document listing your diagnosis, chemotherapy drugs, radiation and surgeries. Your follow-up care depends on exactly what treatment you had, and survivors often don't know those details (Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh (opens in a new tab)).

  1. Ask the hospital that treated you. Contact its pediatric oncology department or medical records office and ask for your treatment summary, or your full oncology records if no summary exists.
  2. Ask about Passport for Care. It's a free online tool from Baylor College of Medicine and Texas Children's Cancer Center. Survivors at participating clinics can see their treatment summary and personalized follow-up recommendations (Texas Children's (opens in a new tab)).
  3. Use the Screenings Recommendations Generator if your clinic isn't signed up. Any childhood cancer survivor can use it to see possible late effects and recommended screenings (Baylor College of Medicine (opens in a new tab)).
  4. Know the guidelines your doctor should follow. The Children's Oncology Group's Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines set the standard for survivor screening and are free online, including plain-language "Health Links" handouts (survivorshipguidelines.org (opens in a new tab)).

Questions to bring to your doctor

Tip: you can print this list and bring it to your appointment. It always prints on its own page, which is the last page, so you can choose to print just that page.

  • Do you have my cancer treatment summary? If not, how do I get one?
  • Based on my treatment, which late effects am I at risk for?
  • Which screenings do I need, and when should they start? For example, heart, thyroid, bone or fertility checks (Blood Cancer United (opens in a new tab))
  • Are you familiar with the Children's Oncology Group Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines?
  • Can you refer me to a survivorship clinic?
  • Who should I call if I notice a new symptom?
  • Can you connect me with mental health or social work support?
